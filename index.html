01 — OVERVIEW

A different kind of airway obstruction.

Palatal prolapse, also called expiratory palatal obstruction (EPO), describes upward or backward movement of the soft palate during expiration, potentially narrowing or closing the nasal passage through the velopharynx. [1][2][4][20]

In susceptible individuals, the mobile soft palate and uvula may balloon into the nasopharyngeal airway as air is exhaled. Nasal airflow can drop abruptly, sometimes redirecting toward the mouth. The tissue can return to its usual position when the pressure gradient changes with the next inspiration. [1][4][5]

A 2026 review of 75 anatomical studies underscores that the soft palate is not a passive single flap: several muscles, the palatine aponeurosis, and their attachments coordinate speech, swallowing, and airway behavior. This anatomy helps explain why collapse patterns may differ between people, but the review does not establish an effective treatment for prolapse. [23]

The phenomenon was described in anesthesia literature decades ago, and similar expiratory obstruction has been observed during bag-mask ventilation and sedation. [6][7][13] Rare cases have also been reported in people with myasthenia gravis while awake. [21]

↗
An important distinction

Palatal prolapse is a dynamic upper-airway phenomenon and is not, by itself, synonymous with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Its clinical significance depends on the broader respiratory and sleep findings. [1]

02 — RECOGNITION

How is it identified?

Palatal prolapse can be difficult to recognize from a routine sleep-study summary alone. Interpreting a suspected airflow pattern requires considering the whole sleep study, the type of sensor, and other possible causes of obstruction. [1][20]

01

Direct visualization

Endoscopic observation of the upper airway during natural sleep directly demonstrates palatal movement and was used to define the phenomenon in the foundational physiological study. Drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) can reveal related collapse patterns in clinical assessments, but sedation may not reproduce natural sleep precisely. [1][5][22]

Direct visualization · most specific
02

Expiratory airflow signatures

A repeated abrupt decrease or truncation of the nasal expiratory flow signal may raise suspicion. In a 2018 study of 22 patients with OSA, a research-derived expiratory flow-limitation index was compared with endoscopic findings. Sensor noise, swallowing, leaks, and other mechanisms can mimic an abnormal trace: waveform appearance alone is not definitive. [1][9] A 2023 conference abstract compared endoscopically observed prolapse with separate oral and nasal airflow measurements in 99 selected patients with OSA; it does not validate diagnosis from home CPAP waveforms alone. [18]

Noninvasive clue · needs interpretation
03

Clinical sleep assessment

Polysomnography and a sleep-medicine evaluation can establish whether OSA, arousals, position dependence, or other sleep disorders coexist. A CPAP download may provide supporting information, but is not validated as a stand-alone test for palatal prolapse.

03 — CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

Why it may matter for sleep.

In some patients, the soft palate's motion is pronounced enough to coincide with arousals or to affect the next inspiration. The original endoscopic investigation associated expiratory palatal prolapse with isolated inspiratory palatal collapse, suggesting that the expiratory pattern can provide clues to the mechanism of upper airway obstruction. [1][14]

A clinical commentary described a patient with a sudden mid-expiratory flapping sensation and disturbed breathing despite few conventional apnea findings. This is an illustrative case account, not evidence that every suspected palatal prolapse episode causes arousal or insomnia. [3]

Some episodes may not meet conventional apnea or hypopnea definitions and may occur without a clear oxygen desaturation. However, palatal prolapse can coexist with clinically significant OSA; it should not be assumed to be harmless on that basis. Whether it independently causes insomnia or specific REM-sleep problems remains insufficiently established. [1][12]

SUPPORTED

A marker of airway mechanics

Endoscopic and airflow studies demonstrate an association between expiratory prolapse, airflow limitation and particular collapse patterns. [1][20]

NOT YET ESTABLISHED

A stand-alone cause of insomnia

Symptom attribution and the benefit of treating prolapse separately from coexisting OSA require more clinical evidence.

04 — TREATMENT CONSIDERATIONS

What can be done?

There is currently no widely validated, palatal-prolapse-specific treatment algorithm. Interventions used for OSA or upper-airway obstruction may have different effects on inspiration and expiration, depending on individual anatomy. [20][22][25]

01

Positive airway pressure (PAP)

CPAP remains an effective, established therapy for OSA overall. In people with expiratory palatal obstruction, expiratory pressure, mask interface, leak and other airway collapse sites may influence the response. Research has documented positive-pressure effects on palatal position. Expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP), expiratory pressure relief (EPR/C-Flex) and bi-level pressure support can each alter airway mechanics, but claims that a particular setting always improves or worsens palatal prolapse are not supported by robust trials. [5][20]

Discuss pressure changes or CPAP intolerance with a qualified sleep clinician rather than independently changing prescribed settings.

02

Body position

Side sleeping and other positional strategies may be considered when positional obstructive sleep apnea is present. Their benefit for expiratory palatal prolapse specifically remains uncertain, so treatment should be based on individual assessment rather than a flow-rate pattern alone.

03

Mandibular advancement devices

A 2020 study found that certain palatal prolapse-related expiratory airflow patterns helped predict reduced likelihood of response to oral appliance therapy in some patients. [9] In a 2026 exploratory observational study of 75 adults during drug-induced sleep endoscopy, greater mandibular advancement improved some inspiratory obstruction but increased supine expiratory velopharyngeal obstruction from 41.3% to 64.0%. This short-term simulation does not establish long-term symptoms or treatment outcomes. [22]

04

Other devices and interfaces

Oral-mask PAP interfaces, nasal expiratory positive-airway-pressure devices, nasopharyngeal stents, and palatal support devices have all been discussed as possible approaches. Examples mentioned historically include Theravent, Bongo Rx / AirAvant, Optipillows, Nastent, AlaxoStent, Back2Sleep, Ronchex, and Velumount. Small OSA or stent studies exist, but evidence specifically establishing their long-term effectiveness and safety for expiratory palatal prolapse is limited. [10][11][17]

Products are listed as historical examples, not endorsements; availability and regulatory status have not been verified.

05

Palatal surgery

Site-directed soft-palate surgery—including uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP), selected reconstruction approaches, and palatal implants (Pillar)—has been discussed for appropriately selected patients with OSA and retropalatal collapse. Implants have their own reported complications. A 2026 trial of 34 patients with selected retropalatal obstruction reported improvements after two palatal procedures, but did not specifically establish resolution of expiratory palatal prolapse. Options and risks require individualized evaluation. [8][16][19][24][25] A 2026 surgical anatomy review likewise emphasizes matching the procedure to the anatomical obstruction pattern rather than assuming one palatal technique fits all patients. [25]

Medication evidence is especially indirect.

Gabapentin and other medications have been discussed in relation to sleep-disordered breathing. Evidence concerning drug-related OSA physiology should not be treated as proof that gabapentin causes or treats palatal prolapse. [15]
